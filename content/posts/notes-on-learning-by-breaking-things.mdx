In Minecraft, nobody reads the wiki first. You punch a tree, you fall in a cave, you meet a creeper, and *then* you read the wiki — because now you have a question that matters to you.

I think that's the healthiest model of technical learning I know, and most curricula get it exactly backwards.

## Curiosity needs a wound to heal

Information sticks when it answers a question you actually have. Breaking something — deliberately or not — manufactures those questions at an industrial rate. Why did deleting this line break login? Why does the build pass locally and fail in CI? Why does my falling block sometimes pass through the floor?

Every one of those questions is a hook. Knowledge hung on a hook stays; knowledge poured over a smooth surface runs off.

## Break things with a budget

The trick is to break things where the blast radius is paid for:

- **Side projects** — this blog exists partly as a demolition site. The TNT is thematic.
- **Branches** — `git switch -c what-if` is a free creative mode.
- **Reproductions** — when a big system misbehaves, rebuild the smallest world where the bug still spawns.

## Keep the crater

The last step everyone skips: write down what the explosion taught you. A bug you fixed but can't explain is a creeper you outran — it's still out there. A bug you can explain in two paragraphs is gunpowder: an ingredient for building something better.

> The wall isn't there to stop you. It's there to make the hole mean something.
